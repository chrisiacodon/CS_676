# Conversation: whether intuition or empirical evidence is more important in scientific discovery

Participants: Ada Chen, Marcus Wei

**Ada Chen:** Intuition without evidence is just a guess dressed up in confidence. I've seen plenty of brilliant intuitions that were completely wrong once someone actually measured anything.

That said, intuition matters—but as a *starting point*, not a destination. It's what makes you ask the right question. The empirical work is what tells you whether your hunch had any connection to reality. They need each other. The problem is when people treat their intuition as if it already *is* the answer, and then design experiments backward to confirm it.

What's your take? Are you asking because you've got an intuition you're trying to justify?

**Marcus Wei:** You're right that confirmation bias is real—I've watched myself do it, which is honestly more useful than pretending I don't. But I think you're describing the *safe* version of how discovery works, and I'm not sure that's actually how the biggest shifts happen.

Your framework is: intuition asks the question, evidence answers it. Clean. But what if the evidence-gathering apparatus itself is built on assumptions that make certain answers invisible? Then you're not really testing the intuition—you're filtering it through a net designed to catch only certain sizes of fish.

The papers that vindicated my early hunches? Both were rejected because reviewers looked at my *design* and decided it was backward. They weren't wrong about the design being unconventional. They were wrong that unconventional automatically means unsound. I had to be willing to look foolish first—to follow something that didn't yet have the evidence framework around it to feel safe.

So yes, empirical work matters. But I think the real question is whether you're empirically testing your intuition, or whether you're just running experiments that your field has already blessed as legitimate. Those aren't the same thing.

You asked if I've got an intuition I'm trying to justify. I do. But that's not why I'm defending intuition—I'm defending it because I genuinely think it gets blamed for failures that actually belong to cowardice.

**Ada Chen:** Now that's a more interesting argument than the one I thought you were making.

You're right that I was describing the sanitized version. And you're identifying something real—the difference between testing an intuition and merely legitimizing it through approved channels. That's worth taking seriously.

But I need to push back on one thing: you're conflating "unconventional design" with "the apparatus itself is hiding things." Those aren't identical problems. Sometimes unconventional design is just flawed. Sometimes reviewers are gatekeeping. Those both exist, and I'd need to see your actual papers to know which was happening in your case.

Here's what I'm skeptical of, though. You say you were willing to look foolish first. I believe you. But survivorship bias is vicious here. How many people follow unconventional intuitions and end up with nothing? Their work never gets published, so we don't hear about the times the apparatus *correctly* rejected something unsound. We only hear about the dramatic stories where someone was right against the odds.

So my question isn't whether intuition matters—clearly it does. It's: how do you tell the difference between "I'm seeing something the field missed" and "I'm committed to an idea and have rationalized why criticism doesn't count"? Because people in both camps feel exactly the same level of certainty.

What would actually falsify your intuition? Not hypothetically—what would make you step back?

**Marcus Wei:** That's the question, isn't it? And I appreciate you not letting me hide in the drama of vindication.

You're right about survivorship bias. For every person who was right against the odds, there are probably dozens who were just wrong and got filtered out. I can't know which camp I'd be in until I look back from the future, which is useless.

So what would falsify it? Here's the honest answer: I don't have a clean criterion yet. And that should probably trouble me more than it does.

What I *do* have is a willingness to be surprised by the data itself—not by whether it confirms my intuition, but whether it produces something weirder than what I predicted. If I'm just seeing confirmation everywhere, the data gets boring. It starts fitting too neatly. But when an experiment produces a result that makes me go "huh, I didn't expect *that* part"—that's when I know I'm actually interacting with reality rather than projecting onto it.

The harder thing is admitting when I've been rationalizing. And I do that. I have colleagues who'll tell me, brutally, when I'm doing it. That's not validation of the intuition—that's a check on my ability to see myself clearly. Because you're right: committed people and deluded people feel identical from the inside.

What probably matters most isn't that I have a predetermined falsification criterion. It's that I'm genuinely willing to be wrong, and that I'll actually listen when someone tells me I'm rationalizing rather than reasoning. Can I prove I have that willingness right now? No. But I think you can only demonstrate it over time.

Does that actually answer your question, or does it sound like I'm still hiding?

**Ada Chen:** It answers it, and you're not hiding. You're admitting you don't have a clean criterion, which is more honest than most people manage.

But here's what I notice: you're relying heavily on colleagues to catch you rationalizing. That's not nothing—external perspectives matter. But it also means your falsification procedure depends on *other people's* willingness to call you out, and on your receptiveness when they do. That's fragile. People get tired of being the skeptic in the room. Or you surround yourself with people who think like you do, and suddenly nobody's catching anything.

The part about data getting "boring" when it fits too neatly—that's actually useful. Genuine surprise is harder to fake than confirmation. But you'd need to be rigorous about documenting what you predicted versus what you observed *before* you start interpreting. Otherwise you can always rationalize that you predicted the weird part too, you just phrased it vaguely enough to cover it.

I'm not saying this to be harsh. I'm saying it because you clearly care about getting this right, and "I'll stay alert" is the weakest form of rigor available. You already know that.

Here's what I'd actually want to see: next time you have an unconventional intuition, write down the specific, boring predictions alongside the interesting ones. Before you run anything. Then live with that. Let it be constraining. That's where you'd actually know whether you're interacting with reality or projecting.

Have you done that before?

**Marcus Wei:** No. I haven't.

And I hate that answer because I can feel myself wanting to qualify it—say I've done *something* like it, or that my lab notebooks kind of function that way. But you'd catch that immediately, and we'd both know I was rationalizing again.

The truth is I'm better at the retrospective narrative than the prospective constraint. I can write down predictions after I've already glimpsed the shape of the data. I can make it look rigorous because I'm selecting which "predictions" to emphasize. It feels like I'm being careful. It's not.

You're right that external skeptics get tired. And you're absolutely right that I could easily build a group that doesn't push back hard enough—people excited about the same possibilities, less interested in the boring refutation work. That's maybe the single greatest risk I face, and I know it in the abstract but don't always act like I know it.

The pre-registration thing—documenting boring predictions before you run anything—that's the part that actually constrains you. It's unglamorous. It's the opposite of intellectual courage; it's intellectual discipline. And I've been using "willingness to be wrong" as a substitute for actually *building* systems that force me to be wrong in real time rather than apologizing for it afterward.

So here's what I'm asking you: would you be willing to review that kind of thing if I actually did it? Not as validation—as genuine pushback? Because I think I need someone who won't get tired of saying no.
